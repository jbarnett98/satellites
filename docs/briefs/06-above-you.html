Above you: an observer on the ground, the sky over their head, and tonight's passes

A visitor can now say where they are — type a city, click the globe, or let the browser tell us — and the site answers the question people actually bring to a satellite map: what is over my head right now, and when will I see something? The pin sits on the globe and everything below that person's horizon fades; a sky chart shows the hemisphere above them, zenith in the middle; the stations, Hubble and whatever they have selected get a 24-hour pass list with rise, peak, set, and whether the pass is visible to the naked eye. All of it is computed in the browser from the snapshot the page already holds — one millisecond per sky scan, forty-odd milliseconds for a day of predictions.

What we just completed

  • The observer's frame, without Three.js. A location becomes an Earth-fixed position and three axes — east, north, up — and, at any instant, those are rotated into the inertial frame by the sidereal angle. A satellite's direction is then the difference vector projected on the axes: azimuth from north through east, elevation from the horizon. The module is plain arithmetic on number triples so the propagation worker uses it too; the WGS84 constants moved into a small file of their own for the same reason.
  • The sky scan. Every time the worker delivers new positions (once a second at real time), the globe walks the position buffer once with the observer already rotated into the renderer's frame: three dot products and one asin per object, 1.1 ms for the catalogue. Out comes a byte mask of what is above the horizon — which becomes the emphasis attribute, so the visible cone brightens and the rest of the sky fades — and a list of (index, azimuth, elevation, range) sorted by elevation for the panel and the chart. Hidden objects (filtered types, debris clouds off) are skipped so the counts match what is drawn.
  • Pass prediction in the worker. For each watched object, elevation is sampled along the orbit at a coarse step (a 180th of the period: 30 s for the ISS, capped at 2 min), each rise refined by bisection to well under a second, the peak by golden-section search, the set by bisection again; passes that never reach the minimum elevation are dropped. Each pass is then sampled every 8 s for azimuth, elevation, whether the satellite is sunlit (the same cylindrical-shadow test the shader uses) and whether the observer's sky is dark (Sun more than 6° below their horizon — the end of civil twilight). Sunlit over a dark sky is "visible", and the first and last such moments are the visibility window. Fifteen objects for 24 hours cost about 59,000 SGP4 evaluations and 40–80 ms; the ISS alone is 3,634 evaluations in 14 ms.
  • The watchlist. CelesTrak's stations group turned out to hold 23 objects: the ISS and Tiangong modules, seven docked vehicles, a rocket stage, two pieces of debris shed from the station, and six cubesats it recently deployed. The list keeps payloads, drops anything carrying the ISS's own designator 1998-067 other than the station itself (that is how deployed cubesats are catalogued), adds Hubble, the selected object, and a picked group when it is 200 objects or fewer — GPS, Galileo, the ISS group — and says so in the panel when it is not. Objects flying together rise and set together, so the ISS and its six docked vehicles fold into one row, "ISS (ZARYA) +6".
  • Setting the location. Four ways: a typeahead over 7,342 Natural Earth places (public domain; a pipeline builder turns the GeoJSON into a 325 KB row list, fetched the first time the panel needs it); a click on the globe, which casts the pointer ray at the ellipsoid and converts the hit to geodetic latitude and longitude; two coordinate boxes; and browser geolocation, rounded to a hundredth of a degree (about a kilometre) so it is a sky, not an address. A picked point is named after the nearest listed place — "Glasgow" within 25 km, "near Lisbon" beyond. The location persists in localStorage and in the link, except that a geolocated position never goes into a link you might share.
  • The sky chart. A 296-px canvas redrawn five times a second: horizon at the rim, zenith at the centre, 30° and 60° rings, the minimum-elevation ring in the accent colour, north up and east on the left — the way a planisphere reads when you hold it overhead. Every object above the horizon is a dot in its type colour; the geostationary belt appears as an arc at the latitude's characteristic elevation. Hovering a list row rings its dot; clicking a dot selects the object. The selected object's next pass (or a pass clicked in the list) is drawn as a track, grey where unlit or in daylight, accent where you could see it, with rise and set times at the ends.
  • Times. Passes are listed in the browser's zone (labelled, e.g. BST) with a UTC toggle; "Today 20:59", "Tomorrow 06:12"; "in 1 h 06 min"; a pass in progress says "Now · until 21:10 · sets in 8 min". The window is 24 hours from the simulation clock and rolls forward once the clock is two hours into it; while the clock is running faster than 60× it does not roll at all, because each roll is a worker pause the propagation ticks would feel.
  • Verified, in the browser, against the real snapshot: Glasgow named from a link's coordinates; 1,045 objects above its horizon at civil twilight; the ISS pass tonight at 20:59 BST rising west, peaking 40° in the south, setting east-south-east, visible until it enters the Earth's shadow at 21:05 — the same numbers the Node run gave before any UI existed; Hubble and Tianhe correctly never clearing 10° from 56° N, and Hubble correctly clearing 36° from Sydney; Progress MS-35, launched this afternoon and still 140 km below the station, as its own row; a click off the Portuguese coast becoming "near Lisbon" with the passes recomputed; Sydney at 04:58 local reported as nautical twilight; the pin and the dimmed cone correct in both view frames; the window rolling at 3,600×.
Working principles honoured

Nothing was fetched from a live service by the browser. The one new dataset is public domain, built by the pipeline, and static. Pass prediction — the classic reason satellite sites run a server — is a few dozen milliseconds in the same Web Worker that already propagates the catalogue.

The dependency rule bends in one sanctioned place: orbits now imports from astro (time, sun, topocentric) — both are pure maths at the bottom of the graph, and the worker needs the Sun and the sidereal angle to judge visibility. Nothing in astro or orbits imports Three.js or Svelte; places sits beside them.

Under the hood: from a location to a pass

Two loops run once the observer is set. The fast one answers "what is up now" from positions the layer already has; the slow one asks the worker to look a day ahead for a short list of objects. Neither touches the frame loop's cost: the scan is a millisecond per tick, and the prediction is a message.

The chart's projection, in one paragraph

Elevation maps linearly to radius (90° at the centre, 0° at the rim), which spaces the 30° and 60° rings evenly and keeps the low sky — where passes begin and end — from being squashed. Azimuth is the angle from north, but mirrored: the chart is drawn as if you were lying on your back looking up, so east is on the left, as on a planisphere or a star map held overhead. That is the convention that lets a person turn from the screen to the sky without flipping anything in their head.

Ground picking, in one paragraph

The pointer's ray is taken from world space into the Earth group's local frame, where the planet is a sphere of the equatorial radius and the polar flattening is the group's scale. The ray-sphere intersection is solved in that frame; the hit point's scene swizzle is undone and its polar scale reapplied to give an Earth-fixed coordinate, which the existing WGS84 conversion turns into geodetic latitude and longitude. The same rotation the Earth mesh carries is what makes the pick land on the right city in both the inertial and the Earth-fixed views.

Immediate next avenues

A

"What can I see tonight?" across the whole catalogue

Prefilter by geometry (an orbit whose inclination band never reaches the observer's latitude cannot pass overhead) and by size (radar cross-section large or medium), which leaves a few hundred candidates; predict those; list tonight's visible passes by brightness proxy. About a second in the worker, once per night.

Turns the feature from "the stations" into "everything worth going outside for"; medium effort.

B

Catalogue explorer: the data as charts

Unchanged from the Stage 5 brief: altitude × inclination scatter linked to the globe, launches by year and owner, the shells as histograms. The sky chart is the first canvas in the app; the explorer would be the second.

The first "data platform" feature; mostly charts and layout.

C

Look up: a view from the ground

A second camera placed at the observer's pin, looking at the sky with the real stars behind and the satellites moving across them — the sky chart, but in 3D and in time. The scene already has everything; it is a camera and a horizon.

Spectacular for the ISS pass tonight; a day of work.

D

Go public

Cloudflare Pages + R2; links become sendable, and geolocation works because Pages is HTTPS. Unchanged from the Stage 4 brief.

About a day; $0/month.

Open questions and risks

  • "Visible" is a lighting statement, not a brightness one. The ISS, Tiangong and Hubble are bright when sunlit; a docked Progress is faint; a GPS satellite at 20,000 km is beyond the naked eye even when the badge says visible. A magnitude model (standard magnitudes for the famous objects, RCS as a proxy for the rest) would make the badge honest for everything.
  • Times are in the visitor's zone, not the observer's. Someone in London looking at Sydney's sky sees BST times, labelled as such. A zone-from-coordinates dataset is a few hundred kilobytes and would fix it.
  • The model ignores refraction (about half a degree at the horizon) and uses a cylindrical shadow (no penumbra). Rise and set are a few seconds off; the shadow entry a few seconds soft. Fine for going outside, not for timing an occultation.
  • Element sets age. Predictions 24 hours out from elements up to a day old are good to seconds for the ISS — until it reboosts, which it does every few weeks and which shifts the following day's passes by a minute or more until the next element set. The pipeline's two-hour cadence bounds that.
  • Geolocation needs a secure context. Works on localhost; will work on Cloudflare Pages; would not on a plain-HTTP host. I could not exercise the permission prompt from the test pane — please try "Use my location" in Chrome.
  • CelesTrak's stations group will keep changing. Vehicles dock and depart, cubesats get deployed; the payload-only and 1998-067 rules are the safeguards. A Tiangong cubesat would carry 2021-035 and slip through — a rule to add when it happens.
  • The 200-object cap on group predictions is a guess at what stays under a tenth of a second. Starlink, OneWeb and the debris clouds are refused with a note; avenue A is the real answer.
  • Panels on a 720-px-tall screen now stop above the status bar (all three: Layers, Groups, Above you) and the object card scrolls above the time controls. On your monitor nothing scrolls.

In the page: Above you in the top bar. Type a city, or Use my location, or Pick on the globe and click (Esc cancels), or enter coordinates. The pin appears; everything below your horizon fades while the panel is open. The chart shows the sky overhead; click a dot to select it. The pass list takes a minimum peak elevation (0/10/20/30°) and a zone toggle; click a pass to select the object and draw its track; Locate swings the camera to your spot. The object card gains a "From <place>" section with where it is in your sky and its next three passes.
