The satellite layer: every tracked object, moving, in the browser

The globe now shows the catalogue. The page reads the pipeline's snapshot, hands the element sets to a Web Worker that runs SGP4 for all 19,247 objects up to thirty times a second, and draws each one as a GPU point that the vertex shader carries forward along its own velocity between ticks. Hover names an object; a click selects it, draws one revolution of its orbit, and opens a card with its live position and catalogue record. Debris clouds, colour by type or regime, Earth's shadow and point size are toggles. The page also notices when the pipeline publishes a new snapshot and swaps it in without a reload.

What we just completed

  • Measured before designing. We benchmarked satellite.js 7 on the real snapshot in Node first: building 19,247 satellite records takes ~100 ms; propagating all of them takes 7–10 ms in plain JavaScript once the JIT has warmed (54 ms on the very first call), 5 ms with the library's WebAssembly build, and 11–14 ms with its four-thread build (thread sync costs more than it saves at this size). Positions from the JS and WASM paths agree to a micrometre. That settled the architecture: one worker, plain JavaScript, no worker pool, no SharedArrayBuffer headers.
  • The loader. Two requests — manifest.json (always revalidated) then the immutable gp-<version>.json it names — streamed so the status bar can show bytes arriving, then parsed and checked for the 17 OMM columns. The page never talks to CelesTrak.
  • The catalogue view. One byte per object for type, regime, debris-cloud membership and the stale flag; counts by class; a NORAD → index map; the filter mask the GPU reads.
  • The propagation worker. Receives the OMM columns, builds one satellite record per object, and on each request runs SGP4 for every object at one simulation instant, writing position and velocity into two transferable buffers that go to the main thread with zero copying and come back for reuse. It also samples one revolution of a selected object on demand.
  • The engine. Decides when to ask: 1 Hz in real time, rising with the time-warp to a 30 Hz cap, at most one request in flight, and an immediate tick on any jump or rate change. Each request is aimed at the middle of the interval it will be displayed for, so the shader extrapolates half an interval backward and forward rather than a whole one forward.
  • The layer. A single Points mesh with a custom vertex shader that moves every point by p = p₀ + v₀Δt + ½aΔt² with point-mass gravity, tints by type or regime, dims objects inside the Earth's shadow cylinder, scales dot size by view depth ((17,000 km ÷ depth)^0.6, clamped 0.6–4×), and enlarges the hovered and selected object. The fragment shader draws an anti-aliased disc with a dark rim so a dot reads on black space and on sunlit cloud alike. Depth-tested against the Earth, so the far side is hidden.
  • Interaction. Picking runs on the CPU with the same extrapolation, projects every visible object to the screen, ignores anything behind the planet, and takes the nearest within 9 px (12 px for a click). A press that travels more than 5 px is a drag, not a click. The hover label follows the pointer; the card shows live geodetic position (proper WGS84 conversion, new in frames.ts), height, speed, sunlit or eclipsed, then the orbit and the SATCAT record with owner and launch-site codes spelled out.
  • Verified in the browser: first load, hover, select, deselect, debris toggle (16,582 → 19,247 shown), regime colouring, Earth-fixed frame, 3,600× (worker at 26 Hz, 9.7 ms per tick), and the hot-swap — a rebuilt snapshot (orbits:rebuild, no fetch) was picked up by the open page and swapped in with the ISS still selected.
  • Your first look, folded in. Points now scale with their distance from the camera (base size at 17,000 km, up to 4× when you fly in close, tapering far out) instead of a fixed pixel size; payloads moved from near-white to cyan so stars and satellites are never the same colour; the card's labels no longer wrap; a spurious re-tick at 3,600× that your 36 Hz reading exposed is fixed (the jump detector's tolerance is now rate-aware); and the Milky Way map was regenerated at twice the resolution with a third of the blur.
Working principles honoured

The browser does the maths; the pipeline did its part hours ago. No new services, no new dependencies beyond satellite.js. Nothing about the layer assumes localhost: it reads the same manifest contract object storage will serve. Credits for CelesTrak and satellite.js joined the status bar.

Under the hood: from snapshot to pixels

Three actors, three kinds of memory. The pipeline wrote the snapshot hours ago. The main thread fetches it once and keeps the human-readable columns (names, owners, periods) for the UI. The worker keeps the propagator's own records and does all the orbital arithmetic. The GPU keeps two small buffers of positions and velocities and does the per-frame motion itself.

Why the shader moves the points

Running SGP4 every frame for 19,247 objects would cost 7–10 ms of main-thread time per frame — most of a 60 Hz budget and more than a 240 Hz frame has. Instead the worker computes exact positions and velocities at one instant, and the vertex shader — which runs on every vertex every frame anyway — coasts each point forward from that instant: velocity times elapsed time, plus a gravity term computed from the point's own position. Between ticks a satellite is on a parabola tangent to its orbit; the error grows with the cube of the elapsed time, and the engine keeps that time short in simulation seconds regardless of the time-warp. The main thread's whole per-frame contribution is one number: Δt.

The same formula runs on the CPU for picking and for the card's live readout, so what the pointer hits and what the card reports is exactly what is drawn — not a separate, slightly different propagation.

Frames

The layer lives under the world group in ECI, as planned in Stage 1. In the inertial view the group is fixed and the Earth rotates inside the shell of points; in the Earth-fixed view the group turns by −GMST and everything stays consistent with no per-object work. The Earth's shadow test and the sun direction use the same world-rotated vector the Earth shader already receives.

Under the hood: the timing model

Two clocks matter: real seconds (how often the worker can finish a tick) and simulation seconds (how far the shader must extrapolate). The engine ties them together with three rules.

  1. Interval by rate. Ticks are spaced 1000 ms ÷ rate, clamped between 33 ms (30 Hz) and 1000 ms (1 Hz). Paused: no ticks at all until the time changes.
  2. One in flight. A new request is never sent while one is unanswered, so a slow machine simply ticks less often rather than queueing up.
  3. Aim for the middle. Each request asks for positions at now + rate × (round-trip + interval ÷ 2) — the simulation instant halfway through the time this frame will be on screen. The shader then extrapolates backward for the first half and forward for the second, halving the maximum Δt.

Error estimates use the third-order residual of the parabola, ≈ jerk × Δt³ / 6, with jerk ≈ 0.01 m/s³ for a 400 km orbit. Even the worst case — 0.35 km at 3,600× — is a fraction of one pixel at globe scale (one pixel ≈ 30 km at the default view). Worker load is measured: 7–10 ms per tick on this PC. Stale sets and decayed objects that SGP4 refuses at a given time are parked at the Earth's centre, where the depth test hides them; the count shows in the worker stats.

What the status bar's "Propagation" row means

1.0 Hz · 8.2 ms/tick · Δt 0.4 s reads: the worker delivered one full-catalogue propagation per second; each took 8.2 ms; the frame you are looking at is 0.4 simulation seconds away from the last exact positions (negative just after a frame lands, because of rule 3). The "Loaded" row is the load timeline for your machine.

What the browser needs

  • WebGL 2 (Three.js r186 requires it) and module Web Workers — every desktop browser since 2023. No SharedArrayBuffer, no special headers, no WebGPU.
  • Memory: ~55 MB main-thread heap after load (catalogue columns, Three.js, textures' JS side); the worker holds ~20 MB of satellite records; GPU-side the layer is under 1 MB.
  • CPU: one core lightly used at 1×; a quarter of one core at 30 Hz ticks. The frame loop's own cost is unchanged from Stage 2.
  • Network: 1.09 MB once, then a 2 KB manifest check every 10 minutes.

Immediate next avenues

Find things: search, constellations, filters

Search by name or NORAD id, group by constellation (Starlink, OneWeb, Kuiper, GPS, Galileo…) and owner, per-type toggles, "only what's above the horizon at a place". All of it is in memory already; this is UI and a few index tables.

Small step, high daily value; no new data.

B

Ground tracks and observer tools

Ground track of the selected object, an observer location (yours, or a click on the globe), next passes with azimuth/elevation and rise/set times, "what's overhead now". Needs the ECI → topocentric maths (satellite.js has it) in the worker and a small "observer" panel.

The feature people actually use a satellite site for; medium effort.

C

Space-Track: the full catalogue and history

A second pipeline client (login, the gp class query for everything on orbit, TIP/decay messages), archived from day one; the public snapshot stays CelesTrak-only until your ODR is approved, then the toggle grows from 2.6k to ~16k debris and rocket bodies.

Data completeness; needs your credentials in a git-ignored pipeline/.env and the ODR question answered.

D

Go public: Cloudflare Pages + R2

The pipeline uploads latest/ to an R2 bucket with the same headers the dev middleware sends; Pages serves the built site; the hourly task keeps feeding it while your PC is on, GitHub Actions takes over when you say. Everything on the page already speaks that contract.

Roughly a day of setup, $0/month on the free tiers; the moment the project becomes a link you can send.

Open questions and risks

Needs your input

The Milky Way. The regenerated map is sharper but grainier — it is a count of 2.5 million Tycho-2 stars, and at this resolution the grain is the stars themselves. If it still looks poor on your monitor, say so and it goes off by default (or out entirely); the only "smooth" alternative is a photographic all-sky image, which you ruled out in Stage 2.

  • Epoch precision. JavaScript's Date keeps milliseconds; CelesTrak's epochs carry microseconds. satellite.js therefore rounds each epoch by up to 0.5 ms — up to 4 m of along-track position for a LEO object. Irrelevant for the globe; noted for any future precision feature (we'd parse the fraction ourselves).
  • Density is honest. 16,582 active objects over a 6,400 km sphere is a dense shell; the depth scaling, the rim and the size slider are the levers. The lines you saw at 3,600× are real: 5,134 Starlinks at 53° inclination and 3,620 at 43° pile up at their turning latitudes (see the chat).
  • Picking walks every visible object. 1–2 ms at 19k; ~3 ms at 35k. Fine while it is throttled to 25 Hz and only runs while the pointer moves; a spatial index is the answer if it ever isn't.
  • The main bundle is 670 KB (177 KB gzipped), almost all Three.js; the worker is 21 KB. Code-splitting is a build-config change when hosting makes it matter.
  • The dev server sends the snapshot uncompressed (4.77 MB). Production serving must gzip or brotli it (Cloudflare does); a binary snapshot format is the next lever after that.
  • Worker saturation on slow machines. If a tick takes longer than the interval, the engine ticks less often and Δt grows; the status row shows it. Nothing breaks.
  • WebGL points have a maximum size (typically 64–256 px); the selected ring at 18 px is far inside it.
  • Objects SGP4 rejects at the current time (decayed sets propagated into the future) are hidden at the Earth's centre rather than removed; the status row's error count is the tell.
