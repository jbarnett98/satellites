What we just completed

Your four notes

  • (a) Drag speed. The orbit speed already scaled with altitude; the scale factor was too generous. Halved it and softened the damping so the globe settles rather than skids.
  • (b) Earth resolution. Downloaded NASA's 21,600×10,800 Blue Marble (30 MB) and 13,500×6,750 Black Marble (8 MB) and resampled them with Lanczos to 8192-wide WebP. The site paints the 2048 tier within about a second and swaps the 8k tier in behind it; the status bar reads "2k" then "8k". Your GPU's texture limit is 16,384, so a 16k tier is possible later if you want it.
  • (c) The night sky. Two layers, both from catalogue data, no photographs. Plotted stars: the HYG database — 119,613 stars, each at its true position, sized and faded by magnitude, coloured by B−V. The Milky Way: the pipeline sums the light of 2,539,913 Tycho-2 stars into a sky-brightness map and the browser drapes it under the plotted stars. The band, the Great Rift's dust lanes and the bulge toward Sagittarius all come out of the data. Both layers have sliders because the right level is a matter of taste and monitor.
  • (d) The Sun. A billboard placed along the sun vector every frame, with the Sun's real angular diameter (0.53°) inside a soft glare. It is drawn in the sky pass, so the Earth occludes it: sunrise and sunset over the limb happen on their own.

And the rest

  • Clouds. NASA's cloud composite as a translucent shell 25 km up, lit by the same sun vector (so it shares the terminator and twilight), opacity 0.42. Clouds also dim the city lights beneath them and cast a faint shadow on the day side, which is what makes them read as a layer rather than paint. They drift east at 0.25°/h relative to the ground — an artistic touch, invisible at 1×.
  • Names. build_stars.py is gone. The pipeline is now a package with modules that say what they do: build_earth_textures_from_nasa, build_star_catalog_from_hyg, build_milky_way_glow_from_tycho2. Data files follow suit: stars-hyg.bin, milky-way-glow-4096.webp, day-8192.webp.
  • Pipeline as a project. First real dependencies (NumPy, Pillow) meant it was time for uv: one pyproject.toml, one lockfile, one command per output.
  • Housekeeping. Stage 1 committed; .gitattributes normalises line endings; the data/ ignore rule was anchored to the root (it had been silently ignoring web/public/data/ too — the star file was never in the Stage 1 commit).
Verification

svelte-check 0 errors. Build 165 KB gzipped. Sirius is the brightest star in both the catalogue (V −1.44) and on screen. The Sun sprite sits over the sub-solar point reported in the status bar. First-paint payload measured at 2.0 MB, full detail 5.5 MB more, all cached after the first visit.

Under the hood: the pipeline

Textures

Pillow opens the 233-megapixel Blue Marble (its decompression-bomb guard has to be switched off for that), converts to RGB, and resamples to each tier with a Lanczos filter, which preserves coastlines far better than the browser's own downscaling would. WebP at quality 86 gives the 8192 day map at 3.1 MB — a quarter of what JPEG needs for the same look. The cloud TIFF is a single grey channel; it stays that way in WebP and the shader reads its red channel as coverage.

The star catalogue

HYG stores right ascension in hours; the module converts to degrees, drops the Sun (row 0), sorts by magnitude, and packs each star into four little-endian 16-bit integers: RA in hundredths of a degree (offset by −180° to fit the signed range), Dec in hundredths, magnitude ×100, B−V ×100. Eight bytes a star, 934 KB for the lot, and the browser turns the buffer straight into GPU attributes. 0.01° is 36 arcseconds — thirty times finer than a screen pixel at the globe's field of view. Names for the 759 proper-named and brightest stars travel in a sidecar JSON keyed by row index, ready for a later hover label.

The Milky Way

This is the piece that earns the 159 MB download. The galaxy to the eye is the integrated light of stars too faint to resolve, so the module reproduces exactly that:

  1. Read all 20 Tycho-2 parts (2,539,913 rows, 4 s in Python 3.14): position, BT and VT magnitudes; convert to Johnson V and B−V with the standard ESA relations.
  2. Exclude stars brighter than V 8 — they are plotted individually, and would otherwise be counted twice.
  3. Weight each star by 10−0.4·max(V, 10). Capping at V 10 stops a single ninth-magnitude star from making a blob; the map then traces the density of faint stars, which is the classic way Tycho-2 all-sky maps are made and matches what the eye sees.
  4. Colour each contribution by its B−V through the same black-body ramp the browser uses, so the bulge comes out warm and the arms bluer.
  5. Bin into a 4096×2048 RA/Dec grid with NumPy's bincount, then divide by cos(dec) so a pixel near the pole (which covers less sky) isn't unfairly dark — this makes the map a surface brightness, which is what a texture on a sphere should be.
  6. Smooth with a Gaussian (σ = 7 px ≈ 0.6°) via FFT — chosen because it wraps around at RA 0° so there is no seam.
  7. Tone-map: subtract the 55th-percentile floor (empty sky becomes black), normalise to the 99.8th percentile, apply γ 0.85, write WebP. 202 KB.

It took three iterations to look right, and the reason the first two didn't is worth recording: flux-weighting produced blotches from individual bright stars, and the browser decodes the map into linear light, so an intensity of 0.12 came out as 38% grey on screen — a fog, not a glow. The fix was the flux cap for the first and a linear-light scale for the second: the slider's 0–100% now maps onto 0–0.3 linear, and the default 17% sits where a dark-sky view would.

Under the hood: in the browser

Two loading tiers

The globe's loader now runs in two waves. Wave one fetches the 2048 textures, the 2048 cloud map, the star binary and the Milky Way map in parallel, builds every object, and reports ready — about two megabytes, so a globe is on screen in roughly a second on a decent connection. Wave two fetches the 8192 day and night maps and the 4096 clouds, hands them to the Earth and cloud shells with setTextures, and frees the tier-one textures. Nothing on screen changes except sharpness. Reload and both waves come from the browser cache.

The cloud shell and the ground beneath it

The clouds are a second sphere 25 km larger than the Earth, drawn translucent after the ground. Its shader lights the coverage value with the same sun vector and the same ±6° terminator as the Earth, warms it near the terminator, dims it to a faint blue-grey on the night side, and thickens it toward the limb where you'd be looking through more of it. The Earth's own shader also samples the cloud map: beneath cloud it darkens the day side by up to 22% (a shadow) and hides up to 80% of the city lights (overcast cities are dimmer from orbit). Both shaders shift the cloud map by the same time-derived offset, so the shadow moves with the cloud.

The sky, in three layers

The sky scene now has a skyRoot group carrying three things in draw order: the Milky Way dome, the star points, the Sun sprite. The dome is a back-face sphere whose fragment shader converts each pixel's view direction into RA/Dec and samples the glow map there — no reliance on sphere UVs, so it lines up with the stars by construction. The Sun is a sprite (always faces the camera) positioned along the sun vector at 95% of the sky radius; its texture is generated on a canvas at start-up from a radial gradient whose inner stop is sized so the bright disc subtends 0.53°. In the Earth-fixed frame skyRoot rotates with world, so the Sun tracks across the sky over a simulated day as it should.

Controls

Orbit speed is recomputed every frame as min(0.6, (altitude / distance) × 0.55): slow near the surface, never faster than 0.6 far out. Damping is 0.06, so a flick decays over roughly a second.

Open questions and risks

Calibrated by eye, on a downscaled capture

Star brightness, Milky Way intensity and cloud opacity were all tuned from the embedded pane's 800×450 screenshots. Your 240 fps monitor at 1:1 is the real judge — the three sliders and the layer toggles are there so you can set the look you want, and I'll bake your numbers into the defaults.

  • Frame rate still can't be measured in the pane; two extra full-screen spheres (clouds, Milky Way dome) and 13× more star points are the additions since your 240 fps reading. I expect no visible change, but please glance at the status bar.
  • GPU memory. The 8k day and night maps with mipmaps take ~170 MB of VRAM; trivial for the RTX 5070, but a laptop GPU with 2 GB would notice. A "texture quality" setting is a natural later addition.
  • HYG is CC BY-SA. The derived stars-hyg.bin inherits share-alike; it is credited in the status bar and README, and the repo will carry the licence note. Tycho-2 asks for citation (Høg et al. 2000), given in the README.
  • Milky Way map artefacts. The rows nearest the poles are noisier (few stars per pixel, amplified by the cos(dec) correction). Barely visible; a pole fade is a one-line fix if it bothers you.
  • Cloud drift is not physical. Real cloud systems move at different speeds and directions. If it ever misleads, CLOUD_DRIFT_DEG_PER_HOUR = 0.
  • Sun sprite versus atmosphere. The Sun is drawn in the sky pass, so the atmosphere halo is drawn over it near the limb — a pleasant accident (it reads as glare through the atmosphere) but not modelled.
  • Fonts still come from Google. Self-host before deployment.
